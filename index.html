    What this link checker actually does

    Most people judge a link by how it starts. That is the mistake. A link like https://paypal.com.secure-login.xyz/verify begins with the word PayPal and ends with a page called verify, so it reads as legitimate at a glance. The only part of that address that decides which website you actually reach is secure-login.xyz. Everything to the left of it is decoration that the owner of secure-login.xyz controls completely.

    This tool takes the address apart, marks the one part that matters, and then runs a fixed list of checks that phishing investigators use by hand. It answers one question: does this link contain any of the structural warning signs that appear in scam links?

    The checks it runs, in order

    1. Real domain extraction. The address is split into scheme, subdomain, registered domain, port, path and query. The registered domain — the part you would actually type to reach the site — is highlighted.
    2. Hidden username check. An @ symbol before the first slash means everything to its left is a login name, not a website. https://absa.co.za@evil.com goes to evil.com.
    3. Lookalike character check. Some letters and numbers are nearly identical: capital I, lowercase l and the number 1; capital O and zero. Scammers swap them so paypa1.com reads as paypal.com.
    4. Brand typosquatting check. The domain is compared against 40 well-known brand names. A match at one or two characters of difference, or a brand name sitting inside a longer domain, is flagged.
    5. Subdomain abuse check. A brand name appearing in the subdomain rather than the domain means the address is trying to borrow trust it does not own.
    6. IP address check. Real companies almost never send links to a raw numeric IP address such as 185.220.101.44. It hides the owner completely.
    7. Unusual port check. Anything other than the standard 80 and 443 ports is rare on consumer websites and common on temporary scam infrastructure.
    8. Unencrypted connection check. http:// rather than https:// on a login or payment page means the traffic can be read in transit.
    9. URL shortener check. Shortened links hide the destination on purpose. The tool tells you to expand it and check the result.
    10. Domain age lookup. The registered domain is sent to the public RDAP registry directory and the response gives the registration date, the expiry date, the registrar and whether DNSSEC is enabled.

    Why domain age is the strongest free signal

    When somebody registers a web address, the registry records the date. That record is public and free to read through a standard protocol called RDAP, which is run by the registries themselves and by the free directory at rdap.org. Nobody can quietly backdate it.

    Phishing works on volume and on speed. An attacker buys a few hundred cheap domains, sends the messages, collects credentials for two or three weeks, and abandons the domains once they are blocklisted. That is why a registration date inside the last 30 days is such a strong warning sign, and why it pairs badly with a second sign: a domain registered recently that also expires within twelve months was bought for one short campaign rather than for a business.

    Domain ageWhat it usually means
    Older than 5 yearsNormal for an established company. Not proof of honesty, but not a warning sign.
    1 to 5 yearsCommon for small businesses and newer services. Judge it together with the other checks.
    30 days to 1 yearNew. Legitimate startups sit here too, so look for a brand name being copied before assuming the worst.
    Under 30 daysHigh risk. Most blocklisted phishing domains are this new.
    Already expiredThe address is up for grabs. Anyone can buy it, including somebody who will rebuild the old site as a trap.

    Some country registries, including several African and Asian extensions, do not publish RDAP records at all. When that happens the tool says the record is unavailable instead of guessing, because a missing record is not the same thing as a safe one.

    How to read a URL, in one paragraph

    Read a web address from the first single slash backwards, not forwards. In https://login.absa.co.za.verify-secure.info/alert the first single slash comes after verify-secure.info, so that is the website you will reach. login.absa.co.za is a subdomain label that the owner of verify-secure.info invented, and /alert is a page on their server. The rule is short: the real domain is the last group of words before the first slash. Everything before it is a costume.

    What this tool cannot tell you

    Read this. A clean result here means the link contains none of the structural warning signs this tool checks for. It is not a guarantee that the destination is honest. This tool does not fetch the target website, does not scan it for malware, does not detect fake login forms, and does not query real-time blocklists of known malicious addresses. Legitimate old domains get hacked and used to host scam pages every day. Keep your browser warnings on, keep antivirus software updated, and when a message claims to be from your bank, type the bank's address yourself instead of clicking anything.

    Questions people ask about suspicious links

    How do I know if a link is safe before clicking it?

    Find the real domain by reading backwards from the first single slash. Then check the domain's age in the public registry. Then look for a copied brand name, a swapped character, a raw IP address, or an unencrypted connection on a page that asks for a password. If two or more of those appear together, treat the link as hostile.

    Does this checker upload the links I paste?

    No. The address is broken apart and checked inside your own browser using JavaScript on this page. The only network request is a lookup of the registered domain name against the public RDAP directory to obtain its registration and expiry dates. The path, the query string and any tokens in your link are never transmitted.

    Are URL shorteners dangerous?

    Not by themselves, but they hide the destination, which is what an attacker wants. Expand a shortened link before trusting it. Several shortening services reveal the target when you append a plus sign to the address.

    What is punycode and why does the tool mention it?

    Domain names may only use basic letters, numbers and hyphens. To allow other alphabets, the system converts them into a coded form that starts with the letters xn--. That coding also makes it possible to build an address that looks like a familiar brand while being a completely different domain, so any address containing xn-- deserves a second look.

    Should I check links from WhatsApp and SMS?

    Those are the two channels where link scams are most common, because both let a stranger reach you with a message that looks like it came from somebody you know. Delivery notifications, bank alerts, parcel tracking messages, job offers and prize notifications arriving through WhatsApp or SMS are the categories to check every time.
